Executive Summary
This document is made possible by the generous support of the American people through the United States
Agency for International Development (USAID). The contents are the responsibility of GEC and do not
necessarily reflect the views of USAID or the United States Government.
GEC, with partner Lodwar Community Health Alliance (LOCHA), implemented the six-month Turkana
Maternal, Newborn & Child Health Strengthening Activity across 14 health facilities in Turkana Central,
Turkana South and Loima sub-counties, Kenya, funded by USAID. Over the life of the Activity, the project
trained 122 health workers in EmONC (emergency obstetric and neonatal care), IMCI (Integrated
Management of Childhood Illness) or essential newborn care against a target of 120 (101.7% of target),
equipped all 14 facilities with essential MNCH (maternal, newborn and child health) kits (100% of target), and
trained and kitted 220 community health volunteers (CHVs) against a target of 220 (100% of target).
All five coverage outcomes closed above target: ANC4+ reached 46% against a 45% target (baseline 28%),
skilled delivery 61% against 60% (baseline 41%), DTP3/Penta3 coverage 81% against 80% (baseline 62%),
caregivers naming three child danger signs 61% against 60% (baseline 31%), and timely KHIS (Kenya Health
Information System) reporting 92% against a 90% target (baseline 64%). Count targets fell short in places:
the project recorded 3940 first antenatal care visits against 4200, 1800 skilled deliveries against 2100, and 78
mentorship visits against 84 (92.9% of target). The officer's report attributes these shortfalls to floods, the
nurses' strike and cold-chain failures earlier in the project; coverage rates reached or exceeded their targets.
Community results stood at 17500 people reached with health education against 18000 (97.2%), 8800
children screened with MUAC (mid-upper arm circumference) against 9000 (97.8%), and 625 children
referred for malnutrition treatment against 650 (96.2%). Activity records show handover of solar fridges and
registers needed more signatures than expected, and the sub-county health management team signed the
asset handover certificates. The project's final recorded next step was handing mentorship and audit tools to
sub-county teams for county-led continuation.


Background and Context
The Activity worked in Turkana County, Kenya, specifically in Turkana Central, Turkana South and Loima sub-
counties, with 14 health facilities and the Community Health Units attached to them. Its participants were the
health workers, community health volunteers (CHVs), pregnant women, caregivers and children served
through those facilities and their catchment communities.
The county context is one where maternal, newborn and child health services started from low coverage
levels. At the Activity's outset, facility records recorded a baseline of 28% of pregnant women making four or
more antenatal care visits, 41% of deliveries attended by a skilled birth attendant, and 62% of children under
1 year receiving DTP3/Penta3; only 64% of supported facilities submitted their KHIS (Kenya Health
Information System) monthly reports on time, and a baseline of 31% of caregivers of children under five could
name at least three child danger signs. These baselines are the recorded starting points against which the
results described in 'Final Performance Against Indicators' should be read.
The Activity addressed these needs through a six-month design implemented by GEC with partner Lodwar
Community Health Alliance (LOCHA), with an approximate budget of 1,150,000 US dollars. The design
combined facility-based strengthening — training and mentoring health workers in MNCH (maternal, newborn
and child health), including EmONC (emergency obstetric and neonatal care) and IMCI (Integrated
Management of Childhood Illness), equipping the 14 facilities, expanding antenatal care, skilled delivery,
routine and outreach immunization, and a transport voucher scheme for emergency obstetric referrals — with
a community system of trained, kitted CHVs delivering health education, danger-sign counselling and
malnutrition screening, alongside monthly facility data reviews to strengthen KHIS reporting. Results were
disaggregated by sex and, where relevant, age, and the Activity contributed to USAID/Kenya's health
development objective. No field evidence files were attached to this section; the context above draws on the
Activity's recorded baselines and design records, and the interpretation that follows rests on those records
alone.

(emergency obstetric and neonatal care), IMCI (Integrated Management of Childhood Illness) or essential
newborn care (101.7% of the 120 target, 71 female and 51 male), all 14 facilities equipped with MNCH
(maternal, newborn and child health) kits (100% of target), and 220 CHVs trained and kitted (100% of target,
141 female and 79 male).

(96.2%). The officer's narrative attributes the missed count targets to earlier floods, the nurses' strike and cold-
chain failures; coverage rates still reached or exceeded their targets. No indicator was flagged as not
calculable.

closed at 81% against an 80% target, up from 62. On-time KHIS (Kenya Health Information System) monthly

Intermediate Result 1: Quality Facility-Based MNCH Services
Facility-based maternal, newborn and child health (MNCH) services strengthened across all 14 supported
facilities, and all service-coverage outcomes closed above target. Over the life of the Activity, the project
trained 122 health workers in EmONC (emergency obstetric and neonatal care), IMCI (Integrated
Management of Childhood Illness) or essential newborn care against a target of 120 (101.7% of target), and
delivered 78 facility clinical mentorship visits against a target of 84 (92.9% of target). Of the 122 health
workers trained over the life of the Activity, 71 were female and 51 were male. On-site mentorship after
classroom training improved use of partographs and neonatal resuscitation, as the training teams recorded
each month.
Clinical training and mentorship.
 Project clinical trainers and nurse mentors ran monthly cohorts, from 14 health workers trained at
Kanamkemer Health Centre in March through the final cohort of 16 at Kalokol Health Centre in August; each
month they completed 14 on-site mentorship visits across the 14 facilities. The August cohort comprised 9
female and 7 male trainees, and the August period result of 16 health workers trained brought the cumulative
total to 122 against the target of 120.
Equipment.
 All 14 facilities received and signed for the full essential MNCH equipment kit — delivery sets, neonatal
resuscitation equipment, weighing scales, fetal dopplers and solar refrigerators where needed — meeting the
target of 14 (100% of target). The final kit was delivered in August, with handover certificates signed by the
facility in-charges.
Antenatal care and skilled delivery.
 Service use rose steadily at the supported facilities. Over the life of the Activity, 3940 pregnant women made
a first antenatal care visit against a target of 4200 (93.8% of target); the life-of-project breakdown records
3940 female and 0 male. Facilities recorded 1800 deliveries attended by a skilled birth attendant against a
target of 2100 (85.7% of target), and 162 women were referred for emergency obstetric care using the
transport voucher scheme against a target of 180 (90.0% of target). Some count targets were slightly missed,
for example first antenatal visits, because of the floods, the nurses' strike and the cold-chain failures earlier in
the project. Coverage nonetheless closed above target: ANC4+ reached 46% against a 45% target, up from a
baseline of 28%, and skilled delivery coverage reached 61% against a 60% target, up from a baseline of 41%.
Immunization.
 Facilities and outreach sessions immunized 2890 children under 1 year with DTP3/Penta3 against a target of
3300 (87.6% of target); the life-of-project breakdown records 1445 female and 1445 male. The project
conducted 174 outreach immunization sessions against a target of 180 (96.7% of target). DTP3/Penta3
coverage closed at 81% against an 80% target, up from a baseline of 62%. Cold-chain failures at two facilities
in June and a national Penta vaccine stock shortage in April contributed to the shortfall in the immunization
count; the count targets were missed slightly while coverage reached its target.
Facility data quality.
Timely KHIS (Kenya Health Information System) monthly reporting reached 92% of supported facilities at
endline against a 90% target (102.2% of target), up from a baseline of 64%. Facility in-charges and records
officers met monthly with the sub-county health records officers, and data quality audits compared registers
with KHIS entries in all 14 facilities; the reporting rate climbed from 66 percent of facilities in March to 92
percent in August.
In summary, 122 health workers were trained and 78 mentorship visits were delivered over the Activity's life,
and service use at the supported facilities rose steadily against every coverage measure: ANC4+ from 28% to
46%, skilled delivery from 41% to 61%, and DTP3/Penta3 from 62% to 81%, each closing above its target.


Intermediate Result 2: Community Health System and Household Practices
Community health capability reached its targets: the project trained and kitted 220 community health
volunteers (CHVs) against a target of 220 (100% of target) and reached 17500 people with community health
education against a target of 18000 (97.2% of target). The final KAP survey recorded 61% of caregivers of
children under five naming at least three child danger signs, up from a baseline of 31% and above the 60%
target. CHVs also screened 8800 children aged 6-59 months for acute malnutrition using MUAC (mid-upper
arm circumference) against a target of 9000 (97.8% of target) and referred 625 children with acute
malnutrition to treatment against a target of 650 (96.2% of target).
Training and kitting followed a monthly cohort model, ending with a final refresher of 10 CHVs in August (6
female, 4 male). Education activity paired CHV household visits with community dialogue days and radio
sessions on antenatal care, facility delivery, immunization, child danger signs and nutrition; the August
sessions alone reached 3200 people (1984 women and girls, 1216 men and boys). Screening volumes rose
sharply in the later months as drought deepened across Turkana South: CHVs screened 1700 children in
June (867 girls, 833 boys), 1700 in July (867 girls, 833 boys) and 1650 in August (842 girls, 808 boys),
referring 120, 125 and 120 children respectively to CMAM sites. Screening days moved to water points and
market days, a shift the activity records note found more children than house-to-house visits during drought.
Women made up most CHVs and most people reached: of the 220 CHVs trained over the life of the activity,
141 were female and 79 male, and of the 17500 people reached, 10850 were female and 6650 male. Peer
supervision by experienced CHVs kept newly trained volunteers active, and handing tools and registers to the
county proved smoother because the sub-county team had joined the monthly data review from the start.
All counts above are life-of-activity results from CHV registers, community dialogue registers and referral
slips; no data-quality flags were recorded against these findings. The sex breakdowns quoted are the
recorded life-of-project splits for each indicator total.


Gender Equality and Social Inclusion
Women and girls made up the majority of participants across the Activity's life-of-project results. Of the 122
health workers trained in EmONC, IMCI or essential newborn care over the six months, 71 were female and
51 were male. Of the 220 community health volunteers (CHVs) trained and kitted, 141 were female and 79
were male, so women made up most CHVs, as the handover team recorded in the final field report. Of the
17500 people reached with community health education, 10850 were female and 6650 were male. Among
children under 1 year who received DTP3/Penta3 over the life of the Activity, the split was even: 1445 female
and 1445 male. Malnutrition screening reached 4489 female and 4311 male children aged 6-59 months out of
8800 screened, and the 625 children referred for acute malnutrition treatment carry no recorded sex
breakdown. First antenatal care visits were recorded only for women, consistent with the service: 3940 female
and 0 male over the life of the Activity.
Monthly training records show women formed the majority in every cohort; for example, the March CHV
training recorded 26 women and 14 men, and the August cohort recorded 6 women and 4 men. Equipment
delivery activities (A1.2) did not record participant sex or disability breakdowns; disaggregated data was not
recorded for that activity. No disability-disaggregated data was recorded for any indicator or activity, so the
Activity cannot report results for people with disabilities. No compliance record on gender mainstreaming or
safeguarding was supplied beyond the recorded sex disaggregation and the handover of sex-disaggregated
registers to the county.
The recorded gap pattern is consistent across results: women outnumbered men among trained CHVs and
among people reached with health education, while immunization screening reached girls and boys in equal
recorded numbers. The officer's narrative attributes this pattern to the composition of CHV cohorts and
community outreach attendance; the records do not document any additional analysis of why these gaps
exist, and none is inferred here.


Environmental Compliance
The Activity completed its environmental mitigation obligations under the initial environmental examination
(IEE) and recorded no environmental incidents during the life of the project. A final waste management audit
covered all 14 supported facilities. The audit found all sharps containers in place and the placenta pits in good
condition. The project's waste management checklist was handed to the sub-county public health officers,
supporting continued county oversight of clinical waste after the award closed. Field report, August 2026
(LOCHA programme team). No remediation steps were required, as the final audit identified no non-
compliance. No separate environmental monitoring evidence files were supplied for this section; the
compliance status above rests on the recorded audit findings and the officer's closing report.


Collaboration, Learning and Adaptation
The Activity worked throughout with county and sub-county health structures, and this collaboration shaped
how delivery adapted. Facility in-charges and records officers met monthly with the sub-county health records
officers for data review and KHIS (Kenya Health Information System) audits in all 14 facilities; timely reporting
rose from 66 percent of facilities in March to 92 percent in August against a 90 percent target (102.2% of
target at endline). Sub-county teams joined delivery directly: the sub-county commander arranged a police
escort for outreach after insecurity near Lorugum, the county pharmacist co-signed pre-agreed equipment kit
lists, and the sub-county health management team signed the final asset handover certificates.
Monthly mentorship after classroom training changed practice. The project recorded that on-site mentorship
after training improves use of partographs and neonatal resuscitation, so trainers paired every training cohort
with on-site mentorship visits to the 14 facilities. Peer supervision by experienced CHVs (community health
volunteers) kept newly trained volunteers active, and linking CHV pregnancy registers to facility ANC registers
helped women return for the fourth visit.
The project adapted its community delivery methods to local conditions. Dialogues timed to market days and
water-point visits reached more men. During drought, screening at water points found more children than
house-to-house visits, and CHV screening days moved to water points and market days. Fixed outreach
calendars shared with CHVs increased turnout at outreach sessions. When flooding cut the Kalokol and
Lowarengak roads, routes were re-planned and children missed during a national Penta vaccine stock-out
were recalled in following weeks. When two solar refrigerators failed, vaccines moved temporarily to the
nearest working cold chain and the units were repaired within eight days.
The project also adapted its handover approach. Handing tools and registers to the county in the final month
is smoother when the sub-county team has joined the monthly data review since the start; the August
handover nevertheless needed more signatures than expected, and the sub-county health management team
signed the asset handover certificates. The final KAP survey fell in a week of hot weather with low turnout in
two villages; the project revisited the two villages to complete the survey.
What would you do differently? The records point to two adjustments: start-up procurement and nurse-mentor
recruitment took longer than planned and partly covered facility services in the first weeks, so earlier
recruitment and pre-agreed procurement would have allowed full service support from month one; and
handover preparation, including signatures for solar fridges and registers, would be built into monthly joint
reviews from the start rather than concentrated in the final month.


Sustainability and Transition
The Activity closed with all equipment, community health systems and supervision responsibilities handed to
the County Government of Turkana, so the gains described in 'Key Achievements by Intermediate Result' rest
on county-owned assets and structures.
Assets transferred.
The project delivered and installed the full essential MNCH equipment kit — delivery sets, neonatal
resuscitation equipment, weighing scales, fetal dopplers and solar refrigerators where needed — at all 14
supported facilities, meeting the target of 14 (100% of target). The final kit went to Lokitaung Health Centre in
August 2026, and facility in-charges signed handover certificates for each delivery. The sub-county health
management team signed the asset handover certificates for the solar refrigerators and registers in the final
month, completing the transfer even though the process required more signatures than expected.
Systems transferred.
 The project handed the voucher register and transporter agreements for the emergency obstetric transport
scheme to the county, the MUAC registers and referral slips to the county nutrition coordinator, the outreach
schedules and final cold-chain inventory to the county, and the CHV supervision checklist after the final
refresher. The waste management checklist went to the sub-county public health officers, and the audit tools
for Kenya Health Information System (KHIS) data quality went to the sub-county records officers. Sex-
disaggregated registers are included in the handover tools.
Responsibilities transferred.
 Mentorship tools passed to the sub-county health management team, which had joined the monthly data
review since the start of the Activity. The officer's close-out observation records that handover ran more
smoothly because of that early, continuous engagement; the handover meeting itself brought together the
County Executive Committee Member for Health, the Director of Health, the sub-county health management
teams and the USAID Agreement Officer's Representative.
Sustainability of the service results themselves — the coverage levels reported in 'Final Performance Against
Indicators' and the community results in 'Intermediate Result 2: Community Health System and Household
Practices' — now depends on the county operating these transferred assets, registers and supervision
routines. The final waste management audit and the marking audit, described in 'Environmental Compliance',
support continued county oversight after the award closed.


Challenges and Lessons Learned
The Activity faced recurring external disruptions across its six months, and each shaped whether service-
count targets were reached. Some count targets were slightly missed — for example immunization and first
antenatal visits — because of the floods, the nurses' strike and the cold-chain failures earlier in the project;
coverage rates reached or exceeded their targets.
Flooding and access.
 In April, long-rains flooding on the Turkwel and Kerio rivers cut the Kalokol and Lowarengak roads for nine
days, two outreach sites were not reached, and a national Penta vaccine stock shortage lasted one week
(activity record, April A1.4). The project re-planned outreach routes around the flooded roads and recalled
children missed during the stock-out in the following weeks. The outreach session logs for April record these
months' field constraints (74b2b5d6-4d1b-415c-9dc7-411a2f2b6b49;
4cf3e302-656e-49ae-8b92-91274ebd5eeb). Life-of-activity results against these indicators are reported in
Final Performance Against Indicators.
Staff industrial action.
In May, a county nurses' industrial action lasted twelve days and reduced routine clinic hours at five facilities.
Project-supported clinical officers ran mobile antenatal and immunization clinics at the affected facilities, and
CHVs in strike-affected sub-counties could not be supervised by facility staff for those twelve days.
Cold chain and insecurity.
 In June, solar refrigerators failed at two facilities and cattle-raid insecurity near Lorugum suspended outreach
for a week. The project moved vaccines temporarily to the nearest working cold chain, repaired the two
refrigerators within eight days, and resumed outreach with a police escort arranged by the sub-county
commander. The June and August outreach session logs record the cold-chain and access constraints behind
the immunization counts (96245166-d716-4208-a8f9-2c4a5a472963; 544b39f3-d99b-4597-
a458-3e4552c2490f).
Drought and distances.
 From July, drought deepened across Turkana South and malnutrition screening numbers rose sharply; some
caregivers walked long distances to reach screening points. The project moved CHV screening days to water
points and market days and organised referral transport for SAM cases with the county. Earlier, some CHVs
missed training sessions because of livestock movements and long distances; some CHVs walked more than
15 km to reach the March training venue.
Procurement and start-up.
 Recruitment of the nurse mentors and the first procurement round took longer than planned, and services at
supported facilities were only partly covered in the first weeks. Procurement approvals under USAID rules
required three quotations and took three weeks, and some equipment items arrived in partial shipments from
Nairobi because of delays on the Kitale road.
Data reporting.
 Intermittent internet and power outages delayed uploads at remote facilities, and some facilities still entered
data late after staff rotations; timely KHIS reporting nonetheless rose from 66 percent of facilities in March to
92 percent in August.
Close-out.
 Handover of the project's solar fridges and registers to the county needed more signatures than expected,
and the final KAP survey fell in a week of hot weather with low turnout in two villages. The sub-county health
management team signed the asset handover certificates, and the two villages were revisited to complete the
survey.
Lessons for future programming.
 On-site mentorship after classroom training improves use of partographs and neonatal resuscitation. Peer
supervision by experienced CHVs keeps newly trained volunteers active, and holding training at the nearest
facility reduces absenteeism. Dialogues timed to market days and water-point visits reach more men. Linking
CHV pregnancy registers to facility ANC registers helps women return for the fourth visit. Fixed outreach
calendars shared with CHVs increase turnout at outreach sessions. Screening at water points finds more
children than house-to-house visits during drought, and screening during routine CHV household visits keeps
referral slips linked to the village register. Pre-agreed kit lists with the county pharmacist avoid duplicate
supplies. Printing the reporting-rate dashboard for the monthly review meeting makes late facilities act.
Handing tools and registers to the county in the final month is smoother when the sub-county team has been
joining the monthly data review since the start.


Recommendations for Future Programming
The Activity closed with all four coverage indicators above target: ANC4+ at 46% against a 45% target
(102.2% of target), skilled delivery at 61% against 60%, DTP3/Penta3 coverage at 81% against 80%, and
61% of caregivers naming three child danger signs against a 60% target. Most count targets were
approached but some were slightly missed: 2890 children received DTP3/Penta3 against a target of 3300
(87.6% of target), 3940 women made a first ANC visit against a target of 4200 (93.8% of target), and 174
outreach immunization sessions were conducted against a target of 180 (96.7% of target). The officer's close-
out field report attributes the missed counts to the floods, the nurses' strike and the cold-chain failures earlier
in the project.
Observation.
 Two practices stood out over the life of the Activity. First, involving the sub-county health management team
in monthly data review from the start made the final handover of tools and registers to the county smoother;
the sub-county team signed the asset handover certificates and the handover was completed in the final
month. Second, location mattered for participation: on-site mentorship after classroom training improved use
of partographs and neonatal resuscitation, and screening and dialogue at water points and market days
reached more children and more men than house-to-house visits during drought. Count-based targets that
depended on outreach were the ones most exposed to external disruption.
Implication.
 Future activities should treat service coverage as achievable even under shocks when community platforms
are already functioning, but should plan larger buffers for count-based targets in flood-prone and strike-
affected periods. Continuity depends on county ownership from the first month, not only at close-out: holding
training at the nearest facility reduced CHV absenteeism, and pre-agreed kit lists with the county pharmacist
avoided duplicate supplies.
Action.
 For USAID, the following priorities are recommended. First, design future MNCH awards so that handover
obligations (assets, registers, checklists) are built into routine monthly coordination with sub-county teams
from month one, replicating the practice that made this handover smooth. Second, allow adaptive outreach
calendars — fixed schedules shared with CHVs and screening at water points and market days — as
standard practice, since these adaptations raised turnout during the drought and after insecurity. Third,
provide contingency for cold-chain repair and outreach rescheduling, given the refrigeration failures and road
flooding the Activity experienced.
For the County Government of Turkana, the priorities are as follows. First, sustain the monthly data review
and KHIS audit practice in all 14 facilities, which lifted timely KHIS reporting to 92% against a 90% target
(102.2% of target), using the reporting-rate dashboard and audit tools handed to the sub-county records
officers. Second, maintain on-site mentorship using the mentorship tools handed to the sub-county health
management team, since mentorship visits supported the coverage gains recorded in this report. Third,
continue CHV peer supervision with the supervision checklist handed over at close-out, and keep CHV
screening days at water points and market days during dry seasons.
