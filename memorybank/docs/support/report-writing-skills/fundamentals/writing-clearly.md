# Writing Clearly for Donors

The best donor reports are read, not merely submitted. A report that is clear, scannable, and well-structured communicates professionalism and makes your achievements easy to recognise. This guide covers the practical writing skills that separate excellent reports from adequate ones.

## Know Your Reader

Before writing a single word, ask: who will read this?

Typical donor report readers include:

- **Programme officers** — The person managing your grant. They know your sector but may not know your country context as deeply as you do. They are looking for evidence that the grant is on track.
- **Senior management or board members** — At larger institutional donors (USAID, FCDO, EU), your report may be read by someone several levels above the programme officer. They need the summary to stand alone.
- **Monitoring and evaluation specialists** — They will scrutinise your methodology, data quality, and evaluation claims.
- **Finance officers** — They will compare your narrative against the financial report. Consistency matters enormously.

Your writing should be clear enough for a senior manager with limited time and detailed enough for a technical specialist.

## Structure for Scanning

Most programme officers receive many reports. They scan first, then read deeply only what catches their attention. Write to be scanned:

### Use Descriptive Headings

Every section and sub-section should have a heading that describes its content. Avoid vague headings like "Programme Progress" or "Achievements." Instead:

- "Measles Vaccination Coverage Exceeds Target by 6 Percentage Points"
- "Supply Chain Disruptions in Karamoja Delay Seed Distribution"
- "Community Feedback Mechanism Resolves 91% of Complaints Within 14 Days"

### Front-Load Your Sentences

Put the most important information at the beginning of each paragraph. This technique, called the "inverted pyramid," ensures that even a scanned sentence communicates its point:

**Weak:** "Following the supplementary immunisation campaign conducted in March, measles vaccination coverage among children 12–23 months reached 91%, exceeding the project target of 85%."

**Strong:** "Measles vaccination coverage among children 12–23 months reached 91% — exceeding the project target of 85% — following the supplementary immunisation campaign in March."

### Use Bullet Points Strategically

Bullet points work well for:

- Lists of specific achievements or outputs
- Simple sequences of events
- Facts that don't need connecting narrative

They do not work well for:

- Complex causal chains (use paragraphs)
- Arguing a nuanced point
- Describing methodology

**Example of good bullet use:**
> Key achievements this quarter:
> - 4,812 beneficiaries received WASH services (target: 4,500)
> - 312 community health workers trained in IMCI protocols
> - 14 water points rehabilitated, serving 8,400 people
> - 91% beneficiary satisfaction rate (surveyed: n=210)

## The Principles of Clear Writing

### 1. Use Simple Words

Prefer familiar words over complex ones:

| Instead of... | Use... |
|---|---|
| Facilitate | Help, enable |
| Implement | Carry out, deliver |
| Utilise | Use |
| Subsequently | Then, after |
| Nevertheless | However, still |
| Endeavour | Try, attempt |
| Comprising | Made up of, including |
| In order to | To |

### 2. Prefer the Active Voice

Active voice is more direct and more honest about who did what.

| Passive (avoid) | Active (prefer) |
|---|---|
| Training was provided to 45 health workers | We trained 45 health workers |
| The intervention was assessed as successful | Our assessment found the intervention successful |
| Resources were mobilised by the team | The team mobilised resources |

The passive voice is acceptable in two cases:
1. When the subject is genuinely unknown: "A data entry error was identified..."
2. When the subject is the evidence itself: "The survey was conducted using..."

### 3. Be Direct About Numbers

Write numbers as numerals for any number above nine. Spell out numbers one through nine.

Exception: If a number begins a sentence, spell it out.

- "We reached 4,812 beneficiaries"
- "Three workshops were held"
- "Twelve thousand people attended the awareness rally"

For large numbers, use thousands/millions notation:

- "3.2 million people reached" (not "3,200,000 people reached")

### 4. Use Transitions

Transitions show the reader how ideas connect. They are especially important in longer narrative sections.

Common transitions and what they signal:

- **However, But, Yet** — Contrast or concession
- **Therefore, Thus, Consequently** — Result or effect
- **Furthermore, In addition, Moreover** — Adding information
- **For example, Specifically** — Introducing an illustration
- **In contrast, Conversely** — Introducing a different perspective
- **As a result, This meant** — Drawing a conclusion

### 5. Consistency in Terminology

Decide on your terms and use them consistently throughout the report:

- If you call beneficiaries "participants" in the first paragraph, do not switch to "beneficiaries" in the fifth
- If you use an acronym like "IMCI," define it on first use and then use it consistently
- If your project has a name, use it; if it doesn't, describe it precisely

## Writing the Difficult Sections

### The Challenge Section

The challenge section is where many reports fall short. Donors want honest accounts of what went wrong. The principle is: **acknowledge, explain, respond.**

**Framework:**
1. What was the challenge? (Be specific)
2. Why did it occur? (Give the cause, not just the symptom)
3. What did you do about it? (Show adaptive management)
4. What is the likely outcome? (Updated forecast)

**Example:**
> "Community health worker retention remained a challenge this period, with a 15% turnover rate in Q2 (annualised). The primary cause was competing opportunities from a government salary increase for community health workers announced in April. To address this, we introduced a monthly performance allowance of UGX 80,000 for top-performing CHWs, funded from programme contingencies. Turnover in July dropped to 6%. The project team assesses that retention risk is now manageable."

### The Lessons Learned Section

Lessons learned should be specific and actionable, not generic.

**Weak lesson:** "We need to better coordinate with partners."
**Strong lesson:** "Joint monitoring visits with government health authorities — piloted in Q2 — produced higher quality data than independent monitoring, as verification was immediate. We recommend scaling this approach to all districts in the next phase, with a joint visit schedule agreed at the start of each quarter."

## Common Style Mistakes

### Acronym Overload

Never assume your reader knows your acronyms. At first use, spell out in full:
> "Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI)"

Then use the acronym for the rest of the document.

### Vague Quantifiers

Replace vague words with specific data:

| Vague | Specific |
|---|---|
| Many beneficiaries | 4,812 beneficiaries |
| Most respondents | 84% of respondents (n=210) |
| Significant improvement | A 23-point increase on the household food consumption score |
| Improved access | 18% reduction in average distance to nearest health facility |

### Unattributed Claims

Every claim needs an attribution. If you state a fact about the context ("Karamoja region has the highest child malnutrition rates in Uganda"), cite the source ("Uganda Demographic and Health Survey 2022, UBOS").

## Formatting for Professional Reports

### Recommended Text Formatting
- **Bold** for key figures and terms on first use
- *Italic* for publication titles and emphasis
- Use only one level of heading hierarchy within sections (never need three heading levels in a short section)
- Left-align all body text (not justified, which creates uneven spacing)

### Table and Figure Formatting
- Every table needs a heading above it: "Table 1: Beneficiary Reach by Sex and District, Q2 2026"
- Every figure needs a caption below it
- All currency should indicate the currency unit (USD, EUR, UGX)
- Percentages and percentages points must be distinguished (a decrease from 15% to 10% is a 5 **percentage point** decrease, or a 33% **decrease**)

### Document Formatting
- Header: Project name | Reporting period | Date
- Footer: Organisation name | Page X of Y | Confidential (if applicable)
- Use consistent date format throughout (recommend: 15 June 2026)
- Never use colour as the only differentiator (accessibility for all readers)
