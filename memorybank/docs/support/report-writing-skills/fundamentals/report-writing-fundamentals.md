# Report Writing Fundamentals

A donor report is more than a record of activities — it is your organisation's primary evidence of accountability, impact, and professionalism. A well-written report builds donor confidence, strengthens your reputation, and increases the likelihood of future funding.

## What Makes a Good Donor Report

A strong donor report has four qualities:

1. **Clarity** — The donor can read it without needing prior knowledge of your organisation or project
2. **Honesty** — It acknowledges challenges alongside achievements, without obscuring facts
3. **Evidence** — Every claim of achievement is backed by verifiable data, indicators, or evidence
4. **Relevance** — It speaks to the donor's priorities, framework, and reporting requirements

## The Structure of a Narrative Donor Report

Most donor narrative reports follow a recognisable shape. Understanding this shape lets you write with confidence and ensures nothing is forgotten.

### 1. Executive Summary (or Overview)

A short standalone section — typically one page — that gives the donor everything they need to know at a glance. Include:

- Project name, reporting period, and date
- Overall progress against the project goal
- Key achievements (2–4 bullet points)
- Critical challenges or risks encountered
- Any major changes to scope or approach
- Financial overview (expenditure rate is always scrutinised)

> **Tip:** Donors often read the summary first and may decide how carefully to read the rest based on it. Make it strong.

### 2. Context and Background

Why does this project exist? What problem does it address? This section grounds the donor in the "why" before you describe the "what."

- Brief description of the humanitarian or development context
- The specific needs or gaps your project addresses
- Your organisation's role and rationale for the intervention
- Any relevant changes in the operating environment since the last reporting period

### 3. Progress Against Objectives

This is the analytical heart of the report. For each stated objective or output, describe:

- What was planned for this reporting period
- What was actually achieved (with specific numbers)
- How the achievement connects to the broader goal
- What evidence supports the claims made

Use the logframe as your guide — every row in the narrative should have a corresponding logframe cell.

### 4. Cross-Cutting Themes

Most donors require explicit attention to:

- **Gender equality** — How did the project address gender-specific needs? What disaggregated data is available?
- **Protection** — Were any protection risks identified? How were they mitigated?
- **Environment and climate** — Did activities have environmental impacts, positive or negative?
- **Inclusion and disability** — Were marginalised groups specifically reached?
- **Do No Harm** — Were any unintended negative effects observed or anticipated?

If your donor has a gender equality policy, you are expected to report against it explicitly — not just as a token mention.

### 5. Challenges, Risks, and Lessons Learned

Donors respect honest reporting of challenges. A report that only shows success raises suspicion.

- What obstacles were encountered during implementation?
- What mitigating actions were taken?
- Are any risks likely to affect the next reporting period?
- What lessons is your organisation drawing from this period?

Frame lessons constructively. Describe what you would do differently — donors want to fund organisations that reflect critically.

### 6. Coordination and Partnerships

Donors increasingly expect evidence of:

- Coordination with UN agencies, government counterparts, or cluster systems
- Inter-agency or intersectoral collaboration
- Local partner involvement and capacity development
- Coordination with national authorities where relevant

### 7. Visibility and Communication

Most grant agreements require acknowledgement of donor funding. Confirm in this section:

- How donor branding was applied (publications, events, communications)
- Any press releases, social media, or visibility activities referencing the donor
- Any communication products (factsheets, videos, briefs) produced this period

### 8. Financial Summary

Always include an overview of financial performance:

- Total budget approved vs. total expenditure this period
- Variance explanation (over- or under-spending, and why)
- Outlook for the remainder of the project

> **Important:** Narrative and financial reports must be consistent. The narrative should explain any significant under- or over-expenditure.

## Writing Style Principles

### Do: Use Active Voice

**Weak:** "It was assessed that the intervention was successful."
**Strong:** "We assessed that 87% of beneficiaries reported improved food security."

### Do: Be Specific

**Weak:** "Many people were reached."
**Strong:** "The project directly reached 4,812 beneficiaries across three districts."

### Do: Connect Activities to Outcomes

A list of activities is not a narrative. Show causality:

"We trained 45 community health workers in integrated management of childhood illness (IMCI). During the reporting period, those workers conducted 1,240 consultations, and child mortality in target communities fell by 18% compared to the same period last year."

### Do Not: Use Jargon Without Explanation

Avoid acronyms and technical terms without defining them. Your donor may have multiple programmes and will not know the specifics of your internal frameworks.

### Do Not: Overstate Achievements

Claim only what you can evidence. Words like "significant," "major," or "transformational" should be used sparingly and with data to support them.

## Common Reporting Mistakes

| Mistake | Why It Matters | How to Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Reporting outputs instead of outcomes | Donors fund change, not activities | Connect each output to a visible result |
| Inconsistent figures across reports | Raises questions about data quality | Cross-check all numbers before submission |
| Vague language ("some beneficiaries," "many activities") | Undermines credibility | Use exact figures always |
| Ignoring negative data | Creates a credibility gap | Report challenges honestly; it builds trust |
| Mixing reporting periods | Confuses donors and auditors | Clearly state the exact period covered |
| Forgetting to attach evidence references | Means nothing without proof | Reference evidence records by ID in DonorDesk |
