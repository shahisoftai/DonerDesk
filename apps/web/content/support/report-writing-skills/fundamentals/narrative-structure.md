# Structuring Your Narrative

A donor report that reads like a list of activities is forgettable. A report that reads like a compelling story — with tension, progression, and resolution — is one that gets remembered favourably. This guide teaches you how to structure your narrative so that data tells a story and challenges are presented with integrity.

## The Story Arc of a Reporting Period

Every reporting period has its own mini-narrative. A good report for period Q2 tells the story of what happened between Q1 and Q2, and where the project stands as a result.

The narrative arc has four movements:

1. **Where we were** (baseline or previous period context)
2. **What we planned** (the intentions for this period)
3. **What happened** (activities, outputs, outcomes, challenges)
4. **Where we are now** (updated position, revised outlook)

### Movement 1: Where We Were

Open with context. This grounds the reader in where the project stood at the start of the reporting period.

> "At the start of Q2, the project had reached 3,214 beneficiaries against an end-of-year target of 10,000. The primary bottleneck identified in Q1 was the late procurement of WASH supplies, which had delayed hygiene kit distribution. Key indicators for water access remained 12 percentage points below target."

This opening immediately tells the reader: where we are, what the problem was, and what indicators show it.

### Movement 2: What We Planned

Describe the plan for the period. This creates the basis for measuring performance.

> "Q2 work plan prioritised: (a) completion of WASH supply procurement, (b) distribution of 2,000 hygiene kits in Districts A and B, (c) training of 45 community hygiene promoters, and (d) baseline survey for the behaviour change communication campaign."

### Movement 3: What Happened

This is the body of the report. Structure it thematically or by objective — not chronologically as a diary.

**Thematic structure (preferred):**
- Section: Water, Sanitation and Hygiene
- Section: Health and Nutrition
- Section: Protection and Inclusion

**Chronological structure (avoid):**
- Week 1–2: Activity A
- Week 3–4: Activity B

Chronological structure is appropriate only for emergency response reports where timing itself is the story.

### Movement 4: Where We Are Now

Close the loop. Show where the project now stands relative to its end-of-project goal:

> "By end of Q2, cumulative beneficiary reach stood at 4,812 — 48% of the end-of-project target — with the distribution campaign now expected to close the remaining gap in Q3. The risk of under-spending has been mitigated through the procurement acceleration."

## Building Analytical Narrative

The word "narrative" in "narrative report" is important. A narrative is not just a description — it is a structured account that makes analytical sense.

### The CAO Framework

For each significant result, use the CAO structure:

**C — Context:** What was the situation?
**A — Action:** What did the project do about it?
**O — Outcome:** What changed as a result?

Applied example:
> **(Context):** Uganda's Karamoja region recorded its worst dry season in 15 years in Q1, causing acute water shortages that disproportionately affected women and girls responsible for water collection. (Action): In response, the project accelerated rehabilitation of 14 hand-pumps in the three most affected sub-counties and established an emergency water trucking schedule serving 6,800 people. (Outcome): Average daily water collection time for women and girls in target communities fell from 4.2 hours to 1.8 hours by end of Q2, and reported water-related GBV incidents in project areas decreased by 31%.

### Causality Language

Make causal links explicit. Use words that signal causality without over-claiming:

| When you mean... | Use... |
|---|---|
| Direct contribution | "the project trained..., as a result..." |
| Partial contribution | "the project contributed to..., in combination with..." |
| Association | "communities that received... showed higher..." |
| Contribution beyond the project | "the increase was also attributable to government health system improvements..." |

Avoid language that overstates your project's contribution:
- **Don't say:** "The project eliminated malaria in..."
- **Do say:** "The project contributed to a 67% reduction in reported malaria cases in..."

## Writing About Challenges

Challenge sections are where donor trust is built or broken.

### The Challenge Framework

For each challenge, answer four questions:

1. **What was the challenge?** (Concrete and specific)
2. **What caused it?** (Root cause, not just symptom)
3. **What was done?** (Adaptive management in action)
4. **What is the updated outlook?** (Honest revision)

### Example Challenge Narrative

> **Challenge:** Community health worker (CHW) attrition in Q1–Q2 reached 18%, significantly above the 10% threshold set in the project logframe.
>
> **Cause:** The Government of Uganda announced a UGX 150,000 monthly salary top-up for CHWs in March, making competing NGO positions less financially attractive. Our existing CHW allowance of UGX 80,000 was below the new government benchmark.
>
> **Response:** We submitted a budget revision to the donor in May requesting authority to increase CHW allowances to UGX 150,000 from Q3 onwards, funded through programme contingency. In the interim, we introduced non-financial incentives (recognition certificates, preferential access to training, mobile phone airtime top-ups) which reduced voluntary departures in May–June to 8%.
>
> **Outlook:** With the revised allowance structure approved, CHW attrition is projected to stabilise at 9–10% for the remainder of the project. Revised CHW-related output targets are not expected to change materially.

### When to Escalate vs. Manage Locally

Donors distinguish between:

- **Operational challenges that the project manages** — Report what happened, what you did, and the outcome. These do not require donor approval.
- **Issues requiring donor decision or approval** — These require immediate notification (often within a specific timeframe in the grant agreement), not waiting until the next report.

Know your grant agreement. Most require notification of:
- Significant changes to project scope, approach, or targets
- Any investigation or allegation of fraud, SEA (sexual exploitation and abuse), or serious misconduct
- Security incidents resulting in harm to staff or beneficiaries
- Any legal proceeding related to the project

## The Art of the Transition

Transitions are the connective tissue of a narrative. They show the reader how one idea leads to the next.

### Types of Transitions

**Causal transition:** "This shortage directly affected the delivery of..."

**Sequential transition:** "Building on the training delivered in Q1, the Q2 monitoring visits..."

**Contrastive transition:** "While activity output exceeded targets, quality indicators..."

**Confirming transition:** "Consistent with the baseline findings, the endline survey..."

**Forward transition:** "Looking ahead to Q3, the project will..."

### Example of a Smooth Transition Between Sections

> "The training of 45 community hygiene promoters (detailed in Section 3.2) equipped beneficiaries with knowledge, but knowledge alone does not change behaviour. As described in Section 3.3 below..."

## Handling Multiple Donors or Outputs

When a project has multiple donors with different reporting requirements, the temptation is to write one report that tries to satisfy everyone. Resist this.

### For Each Report, Ask:
- Who is the primary audience?
- What specific framework or logframe does this donor use?
- What additional requirements (gender policy, environmental safeguards, visibility) does this donor have that others may not?

Write the report to the specific donor, not to a generic template. You can use annexes to avoid duplication.

## The Closing Paragraph

End the report with the same clarity you opened it. The closing should:

1. Summarise the period's overall performance in 2–3 sentences
2. State the project's updated overall position
3. Signal what the next period will prioritise

> "Q2 marked a turning point for the project, with the resolution of the WASH supply chain bottleneck enabling full-scale activity delivery for the first time since project inception. Cumulative beneficiary reach of 4,812 (48% of target) is ahead of the revised trajectory of 4,500 agreed with the donor in the Q1 revision. Q3 will prioritise the hygiene behaviour change campaign and the final round of infrastructure completion, with the project assessed as on track to meet its end-of-year targets."

## Common Narrative Structure Mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence |
|---|---|
| No opening context | Reader doesn't know where the project stands |
| Chronological instead of thematic | Report feels like a diary, not an analytical document |
| No causal links | Activities listed but not explained |
| Ignoring negative data | Creates credibility gap when auditors find it |
| No revised outlook | Leaves the donor without a sense of trajectory |
| Mixing reporting periods | Confuses auditors and programme officers |
| Over-long sections on one topic | Imbalance suggests other areas are weak |
